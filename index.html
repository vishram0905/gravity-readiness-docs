    The problem

    Leaving the vehicle is the first task after landing, and balance is then at its worst.

    Balance is impaired after a gravity transition

    The vestibular system adapts to weightlessness. On return to gravity, balance with eyes closed falls to a fraction of its preflight level and recovers over days.

    Readiness changes by the hour

    A single test gives a provisional classification of balance at that moment. The crew surgeon also needs to know when a retest is worthwhile, and the egress decision remains a clinical judgement.

    No ground team on the Moon or Mars

    After an ISS return, a recovery team assists the crew. On a lunar or Martian surface, the crew must judge readiness themselves.

    How it works

    Measure, score, decide, forecast.

    1. 1

      Measure

      Three 20-s standing trials on a force plate in each of four sensory conditions: firm floor and foam, eyes open and closed.

    2. 2

      Score

      Each trial is converted into the NASA equilibrium score and expressed as a share of the crew member's own preflight baseline.

    3. 3

      Decide

      Seven numbered rules, applied in a fixed order, return GO, CAUTION, NO-GO or CANNOT ASSESS. Overall GO requires two GO sessions.

    4. 4

      Forecast

      If the result is not GO, the NASA crew recovery model gives the time at which every condition is expected to reach its GO threshold.

    Overview

    Overview¶

    After International Space Station (ISS) missions, crew balance with eyes closed on a sway-referenced support had a mean equilibrium score of 74.9 before flight, and the NASA recovery model estimates 13.2 at landing (n = 37, Wood et al. 2015). In a separate NASA study of 11 first-time astronauts tested 2–5 h after Space Shuttle landing, 20 of 22 eyes-closed trials with prescribed head movements ended in a fall (Jain et al. 2010). NASA lists the Risk of Altered Sensorimotor/Vestibular Function Impacting Critical Mission Tasks among the risks of human spaceflight.

    The Gravity-Transition Readiness Model addresses the first task after landing: leaving the vehicle. It converts a standing-balance test into one of four results, GO, CAUTION, NO-GO or CANNOT ASSESS, names the rule that produced it and states the next action. When the result is not GO, it estimates the retest time from the published NASA crew recovery model.

    The model is deterministic. The thresholds combine published values, reference data from healthy adults and stated design choices, each documented with its source, and every result can be traced to a numbered rule. No model is trained, because no public dataset known to us labels individuals as fit or unfit to leave a vehicle.

    What the application provides¶

    • An interactive model. The user sets the inputs of a balance test, and the result, the rule applied and the recommended action update immediately.
    • Explicit safety rules. Seven numbered rules, applied in a fixed order. A fall always yields NO-GO, an invalid recording yields CANNOT ASSESS, and overall GO requires two GO sessions at least 2 h apart.
    • A retest estimate. The time at which typical NASA crew recovery brings every condition to its GO threshold, with a range from the published confidence limits.
    • A comparison with typical NASA crew. Each recorded session is placed on the NASA recovery curve of each condition.
    • Traceable data. Every cited value is listed with its source and page, and every simulated result is labelled as simulated.

    The output supports the crew surgeon and does not constitute clearance.
